Biological symmetries

From C to I — the symmetry groups a protein shell is allowed to have

structural biology
symmetry
viruses
group theory
Published

July 7, 2026

A virus has almost no genome. It cannot afford to encode a large, bespoke shell, so it encodes one small protein and builds the shell by repeating that protein many times. Repetition with every copy in an identical environment is symmetry, and this forces a short, closed list of shapes a capsid is allowed to take. This post builds that list up from scratch, one interactive at a time, from the single-axis groups (C) to the icosahedral group (I) that most spherical viruses land on, and then to the one place the list stops being finite. Each figure is a live widget: drag to rotate, toggle the axes, sweep the parameters.

1. A symmetry is an operation that changes nothing

A symmetry of a shape is an operation you can apply that leaves it looking identical. Rotate a square by 90 degrees and you cannot tell it moved; by 45 and you can. The object of interest is not any single rotation but the whole set of operations that leave the shape fixed. Drag the square below, or step it by 90; the coloured corners give away which operation happened even when the outline does not.

2. Those operations form a group

Collect every operation that fixes a shape. The collection always has three properties: closure (one valid operation followed by another is again one of them), an identity (doing nothing), and inverses (every operation is undone by another in the set). A set closed under an associative composition, with identity and inverses, is a group. The only operation that matters is composition, “do this one, then that one,” and it is what the widget below lets you play with on a five-fold pinwheel: pick two rotations and watch their composition land on a third one that is already in the set.

The chiral (rotation-only) point groups in three dimensions are a finite, complete list, and they get short labels: Cn, Dn, T, O, I. The rest of the post is those five, in order.

3. Cn — one axis

The simplest group has a single rotation axis. Cn is generated by one rotation through 1/n of a turn: a starfish is C5, a three-blade fan is C3. Its order (the number of operations) is just n. Sweep n below, press apply rotation to step by one 1/n turn, and note the assembly falls back onto itself.

4. Dn — an axis and a belt of two-folds

Add to the n-fold axis a set of n two-fold axes perpendicular to it and you get the dihedral group Dn: two Cn rings stacked face to face, related by the perpendicular flips. Its order is 2n. The chaperonin GroEL is the textbook case, two seven-membered rings back to back, D7. Toggle the principal axis and the belt of two-folds separately.

5. T, O, I — the polyhedral groups

Beyond the single-axis families there are exactly three groups built from multiple high-fold axes, the rotation groups of the Platonic solids: tetrahedral T (order 12), octahedral O (order 24), and icosahedral I (order 60). There is nothing between or beyond them; you cannot assemble a chiral point group that mixes, say, five-fold and four-fold axes outside these specific arrangements. Switch between the three solids, toggle each axis family on and off, and read the order and the axis inventory update live.

6. Inside I: three axis families, sixty operations

The 60 rotations of I sort into three axis types, one at each kind of special point on an icosahedron.

  • five-fold (C5) through each vertex: 6 axes (12 vertices), 4 non-trivial rotations each -> 24
  • three-fold (C3) through each face centre: 10 axes (20 faces), 2 each -> 20
  • two-fold (C2) through each edge midpoint: 15 axes (30 edges), 1 each -> 15

That is 24 + 20 + 15 = 59 non-identity rotations, plus the identity = 60, the order of I. Each axis runs through the centre and exits at an identical point on the far side, so “6 vertices” means 6 axes through 12 vertices, and so on. Toggle the families below; turn on capsomers to drop the 60-subunit shell onto the five-fold vertices, which is exactly where this is heading.

Why the mirror-image group Ih does not apply

The group with 120 elements, Ih, pairs each rotation with a mirror/inversion partner. That is the symmetry of a geometric soccer ball or a C60 buckyball. Protein subunits are built from L-amino acids and cannot be superimposed on their mirror images, so a capsid can only ever use the proper-rotation group I (60 elements), never Ih. Chirality is what keeps biology in the rotation-only column.

7. Is the set bounded? Yes, until you allow a screw

For closed shells the answer is clean, and it is the elegant part. Because subunits are chiral, every group containing a reflection is forbidden. What survives is exactly the finite chiral point groups, a complete classified list:

\[ \mathrm{C}_n,\quad \mathrm{D}_n,\quad \mathrm{T},\quad \mathrm{O},\quad \mathrm{I}. \]

There is nothing else. Geometry genuinely bounds biology here: ferritin is an octahedral 24-mer (O), GroEL is D7, and the large majority of spherical viruses are icosahedral (I) (Crick and Watson 1956).

The finiteness breaks in exactly one way. A screw axis — rotate a little, translate a little, repeat — is not a finite point group. You can step along it indefinitely, so it is an infinite group. Rod-shaped viruses such as tobacco mosaic virus and cytoskeletal filaments such as actin and microtubules live here. Bounded and finite for closed shells; unbounded the moment a translation enters. Many bacteriophages are both at once: an icosahedral head on a helical tail.

8. Why I wins, and where capsomers come from

Among the finite chiral groups I has the largest order, 60. One asymmetric protein subunit, copied by all 60 rotations, builds a closed shell of 60 identical subunits — each in a geometrically identical environment — enclosing near-maximal volume for a single gene’s worth of protein. No other point group gives 60 identical positions on a closed, roughly spherical surface, which is why evolution keeps rediscovering it (Zandi et al. 2004). The logic is a chain: tiny genome -> few small proteins -> heavy reuse -> reuse demands identical environments -> identical environments mean symmetry -> the richest closed-sphere symmetry is icosahedral -> the copies cluster into capsomers.

The whole input: one asymmetric unit of adeno-associated virus 2 (PDB 1LP3) — a single VP3 capsid protein, one gene’s worth of protein. The 60 rotations of I copy exactly this subunit into the full T = 1 shell (section 10). Drag to rotate.

Capsomers are the visible surface clusters, and they sit on the axes: pentamers on the C5 vertices (always exactly 12 of them) and hexamers on the faces near the local six-fold positions. The minimal shell is 60 subunits, all pentamers, 12 capsomers: the T = 1 capsid you toggled on above.

On “local six-fold”

The six-fold character of a hexamer is quasi-symmetry, not a true six-fold axis of the whole capsid. The global group stays I — there is no genuine six-fold rotation that fixes an icosahedral shell. The hexamer only looks six-fold locally.

9. The T-number and quasi-equivalence

Sixty subunits makes a small shell. Bigger viruses need more copies, but you cannot place more than 60 in strictly identical environments, because 60 is the order of the group. Quasi-equivalence (Caspar and Klug 1962; Johnson and Speir 1997) is the escape: subunits can occupy nearly identical environments if allowed to flex a little. The shell then holds 60T subunits, where the triangulation number is

\[ T = h^2 + hk + k^2, \qquad h,k \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}, \]

giving the allowed values T = 1, 3, 4, 7, 13, 21, …

What h, k and T count

h and k are the two integer steps you take on a triangular lattice of subunits to get from one five-fold centre to the next; the triangle they span is one of the 20 icosahedral faces. T is the number of smaller triangles that face is divided into, hence the number of subunits per face is 3T and the whole shell has 60T. There are always 12 pentamers (the 12 five-fold vertices) and 10(T − 1) hexamers, for 12 + 10(T − 1) capsomers total. When h and k differ and neither is zero the construction is chiral — T = 7 comes in laevo and dextro hands.

Set h and k on the lattice below, or jump to a preset, and read the counts. The shaded triangle is one icosahedral face; orange dots are five-fold (pentamer) positions, teal are six-fold (hexamer). Flip hand mirrors (h,k) to show the laevo/dextro pair.

The lattice above is one face of the shell. Wrap twenty copies onto an icosahedron and you have the capsid. In the viewer below each capsomer is placed by mapping the same (h,k) lattice onto the twenty faces and projecting to the sphere: 12 orange pentamers pinned to the five-fold vertices, teal hexamers filling everywhere else. Sweep T and watch the hexamer field grow between the fixed 12; flip hand swaps the laevo/dextro pair for chiral T.

You always need both pentamers and hexamers, and a sphere can never close with pentagons alone: by Euler’s theorem, wrapping a hexagonal sheet onto a ball requires exactly 12 pentagons — the same reason a soccer ball has exactly 12 black patches.

10. The structures themselves

The schematic viewers above are the geometry; these are the molecules. Each is a real deposited structure loaded as its biological assembly and coloured per symmetry copy, so the individual subunits — the capsomers — read directly. They stream in as you scroll (the 3D engine is a few megabytes), and each maps onto one of the groups built up above. Drag to rotate.

First the payoff of sections 6 and 8: the single VP3 subunit shown earlier, copied by all 60 rotations of I into a closed shell (Xie et al. 2002).

Adeno-associated virus 2 (PDB 1LP3), biological assembly — the T = 1 icosahedral capsid. 60 copies of the one subunit from section 8, each a different colour. This is the group I made physical: the schematic T = 1 capsid of section 9, now atoms.

Then the other finite groups from the bounded list — an axis with a belt of two-folds (Dn) and a Platonic cage (O).

The chaperonin GroEL (PDB 1GRL): two seven-membered rings stacked back to back — dihedral D7, 14 subunits. One colour per subunit; the seven-fold axis runs vertically between the rings.

Ferritin (PDB 1FHA): a 24-subunit iron-storage cage with octahedral symmetry O. The 24 copies, coloured individually, tile a closed shell on the cube/octahedron axes rather than the icosahedral ones.

And the escape hatch of section 7 — an open helix rather than a closed shell, the one case where the symmetry group is infinite.

Tobacco mosaic virus (PDB 2TMV): a helical rod, not a point group. The deposited assembly is a 49-subunit segment of an unbounded screw — rotate-and-rise, repeated without end, the infinite group from section 7.
